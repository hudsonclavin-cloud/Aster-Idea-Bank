Burn Out

Idea 37 found the point where an impulsive kick would send the rocket off along its departure asymptote. Real engines take minutes: the S-IVB burned for 351 s to leave for the Moon, swinging about 28° round the Earth as it went. This page flies the burn itself, with the thrust on, the mass falling, gravity bending the path and a steering law, and solves the ignition time so the hyperbola still leaves along the asymptote. It says what that costs against the impulsive figure, how the cost grows as the engine gets weaker, what splitting the burn at successive perigees buys back, how much payload the stage can still send, and checks itself against Apollo 17's own translunar burn.

    How this works
    
    
      
      
    • The burn. Position, velocity and mass are integrated together (RK4, 1 s steps): gravity is idea 37's (Aster's GM plus the Earth's J2, WGS-84), thrust is the engine's vacuum thrust along the steering direction, and the mass falls at the engine's flow. The cutoff comes when the energy reaches the target C3, found inside the step by bisection. The energy counted is the one that stays constant on the coast afterwards (point mass plus J2), so the C3 reached is the C3 at infinity; the usual two-body C3 at cutoff, idea 37's and Aster's convention, is shown beside it.
      • 
      
    • Where to light it. The burn starts before idea 37's impulsive departure point and ends after it. The ignition time, and a small constant yaw out of the plane, are solved together so that the hyperbola, followed with J2 out to 20 Earth radii, leaves exactly along idea 37's asymptote.
      • 
      
    • The loss. The delta-v the engine spends (exhaust velocity times the log of the mass ratio) less the impulsive delta-v at idea 37's departure point (with J2's potential counted there). It grows as the inverse square of thrust-to-weight while burns are short, then much faster: a long burn throws thrust away climbing and turning.
      • 
      
    • Perigee burns. Split the departure into N burns at successive perigee passes: the first raise the apogee by equal shares of the impulsive delta-v, the last leaves. Each burn is shorter, so the loss falls; but the phasing takes hours, J2 turns the plane meanwhile, and from idea 37's plane the last burn must yaw that out. The curve on the chart assumes the launch was aimed for the phasing; the tiles give both.
      • 
      
    • The payload. The largest payload the stage can push to the C3 with the propellant it has, finite burn included, found by re-solving the burn for each trial payload; beside it the impulsive figure from the rocket equation. The ascent is not re-flown: a lighter payload would also have reached orbit with more propellant, so this is a lower bound.
      • 
      
    • The checks. scipy (DOP853, SLSQP) flies the same burns independently: the integrator agrees to under a micrometre at cutoff, and the best steering the oracle finds (a 16-knot optimal-control solution, converged by 8) is 0.03 to 0.6 m/s better than this page's optimised law. Apollo 17's translunar burn, replayed from the flight report, comes out 0.11 s short of the 351.0 s it burned.
      • 
      
    • What it is not. No start or stop transients for the chain's engine, no venting, no throttling, no drag at perigee, no Moon or Sun; the asymptote is held fixed, even when perigee burns move the departure hours later. The README has the tests, the oracle and every source.
