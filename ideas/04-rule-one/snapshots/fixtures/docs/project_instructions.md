Reaching low Earth orbit requires roughly 9.3–9.5 km/s of Δv once gravity losses and aerodynamic drag are included. Because the required Δv is two to three times the exhaust velocity of the best chemical engines (~3.0–4.5 km/s), the mass ratio is brutally exponential: typically 90–95% of a rocket on the launch pad is propellant, leaving only a few percent for payload. Every kilogram of payload drags dozens of kilograms of propellant and structure with it.

The skyhook attacks this equation at its weakest point. **The exponential is in the exponent.** If a tether tip can meet our launch vehicle at, say, 3.5 km/s slower than orbital velocity, the vehicle's required Δv drops from ~9.4 km/s to ~5.9 km/s or less. Because of the exponential nature of the rocket equation, cutting Δv by ~37% can cut the propellant fraction so dramatically that payload fractions jump from ~3% to 15–25%, and fully reusable single-stage or simple two-stage vehicles become trivially easy to engineer instead of nearly impossible. A modest subtraction in the exponent produces an enormous multiplication in delivered payload per launch dollar.
