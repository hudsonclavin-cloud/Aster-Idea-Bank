Touch and Go

Idea 43 gave the asteroid its shape and picked a site. This page flies the spacecraft down to it and touches it, the way OSIRIS-REx touched Bennu on 20 October 2020: a departure burn, a Checkpoint burn 125 m up, a Matchpoint burn that matches the turning ground, a fall at 10 cm/s, a few seconds on the surface, and a back-away burn. Every leg flies under idea 43's exact gravity with the body turning. OSIRIS-REx's own reconstructed descent is the test.

          How this works

          The descent

          Three impulsive burns, after OSIRIS-REx (Berry et al. 2020, AAS 20-088): a departure burn aims at the Checkpoint, a point above the site along its normal; at the Checkpoint a burn, and ten minutes later a Matchpoint burn, are solved together (Newton's method through the exact flights) so that the spacecraft's reference point arrives the arm's length above the site, moving straight down at the contact speed with no sideways slide over the turning ground. The Matchpoint's height comes out of that, it is not set. Every leg flies under idea 43's constant-density polyhedron (Werner and Scheeres 1997) with the body turning (Bennu's pole and prime meridian; Apophis tumbling by Euler's equations) and idea 42's sunlight. A leg that passes through the shape is refused.

          Where it lands

          From Checkpoint on, OSIRIS-REx's onboard navigation knew its state to 1 m and 1 mm/s (3 sigma), and its burns carried the execution errors of Berry et al. 2021, Table 2. Those errors are carried to the contact by state transition matrices taken by finite differences of the exact flights, then onto the site plane: a 3-sigma ellipse (the tiles give its semi-axes), the contact speeds' spread, and the chance of landing inside the safe radius. The tests check the linear result against a Monte Carlo of the full flights.

          Contact

          NASA reported that the sampler head met Bennu at 10 cm/s, sank 48.8 cm in about 6 s, and was still sinking when the back-away fired. A constant deceleration a fits that: 0.1 × 6 − a × 36 / 2 = 0.488, so a = 0.0062 m/s². On the 32 cm head and a spacecraft of 880 to 2,110 kg (its dry and launch masses; its mass at contact is not in these sources), that is 69 to 165 Pa of resistance. The page carries that range to other spacecraft and other bodies, labelled as an analog. The arm's spring, the gas jet and tipping are not modelled.

          Bouncing and holding down

          A lander that stays on rigid ground rebounds with the restitution you set, flies ballistically under the shape as it turns, and lands again until it settles or escapes. Holding down is statics: weight is mass times the acceleration a grain feels at the site (idea 43); a push beyond it lifts the spacecraft unless thrusters push back, at force / (Isp g0) of propellant.

          The sample

          OSIRIS-REx's sampleability algorithm (Walsh et al. 2022) estimates what TAGSAM collects from the particle sizes at the site, and cuts it for tilt by an exponential that halves at 3.7°. After TAG its authors ran it for the exact spot touched, from the pre-TAG particle maps: the page uses those published numbers and the tilt function; the zero-tilt numbers are the published ones divided by the 0.434 rock-tilt efficiency. The TAG images showed the head flush, so the authors favour no tilt (258 g, low mobility); 121.6 g came home. For a body with no particle map the mass is refused, and the Nightingale analog is labelled as such.

          What it does not do

          No hazard map (the 25 m plates are coarser than the 8 m safe radius); no onboard guidance updates after Checkpoint; no gravity, sunlight or attitude errors in the dispersions; burns are impulsive; contact is one-dimensional with a constant resistance; anchors that rely on soil strength are refused; nothing about the gas jet, contact pads or the sample's leakage.
