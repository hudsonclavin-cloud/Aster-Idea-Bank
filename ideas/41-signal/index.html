Signal

Idea 40 flew the cruise; this page listens to it. Every day from the departure to the end of a stay at the asteroid it works out how far away the spacecraft is and how long its signal takes, where it sits against the Sun, which Deep Space Network complex sees it and for how long, and what a real spacecraft's radio closes at through a 34-m or 70-m antenna, line by line the way JPL writes a link budget. Near solar conjunction the corona scintillates the signal and the Sun heats the receiver: those days are blacked out, not guessed.

          How this works

          Distance, light time and the Sun

          Idea 40's cruise (every force, 2-hour nodes) and, after the arrival, the asteroid flown on by idea 40's propagator, interpolated by cubic Hermite. Each day at 00:00 UTC the signal received at the Earth's centre left the spacecraft one light time earlier, iterated to a nanosecond (JPL Horizons' definition); the round trip is an up-leg and a down-leg. The Sun-Earth-probe angle (SEP) is measured at the Earth; the ray's closest approach to the Sun gives the elongation the corona sees.

          The ground stations

          One 34-m beam-waveguide antenna per complex (DSS-26 Goldstone, DSS-36 Canberra, DSS-56 Madrid) or the three 70-m antennas, at their ITRF93 positions (810-005 module 301), turned by the Earth rotation angle (idea 40's model). Each day the complex that sees the spacecraft highest takes one pass of the chosen length centred on its culmination, above the elevation limit (10 deg is the DSN's low-power transmit limit). Gain and noise temperature come from 810-005 modules 104 and 101, Appendix A: vacuum gain against elevation and frequency, antenna-microwave noise, and the atmosphere's loss and noise at the chosen weather.

          The link

          Transmitter power less circuit loss plus antenna gain is the EIRP; the free-space loss is 20 log(4πdf/c); the ground adds its gain and loses pointing (0.1 dB), polarization and atmosphere. More than −85 dBm into a 34-m antenna's HEMT (module 104) or −80 dBm into a 70-m antenna's LNA (module 101) overloads the receiver. Noise is k times the system temperature, including the Sun's noise in the beam within 4 deg (module 105). A residual carrier at modulation index θ keeps cos²θ of the power for the carrier, whose loop needs 10 dB of SNR (module 202), and gives sin²θ to the data. A rate closes when the received Pt/No exceeds what it needs (the spacecraft's published threshold where the article prints one, otherwise the rate plus the coding's required Eb/No plus losses) by the margin. "Any rate" solves for the highest rate, up to the DSN decoder's limit (module 208).

          Solar conjunction

          The corona scintillates the signal: module 106's index m (eq. 7) rises to 1 near the Sun, and telemetry frame errors rise sharply once m reaches 0.3, about 2.2 deg from the Sun at X band and 1.0 deg at Ka. Those days are blacked out. Inside 0.35 deg the Sun's own noise in a 34-m beam has no expression (over 300 K); for the 70-m antennas it is only a curve within 4 deg, so those days are refused. The corona's group delay (eqs. 1 and 2, integrated along the ray) is reported for ranging.

          What it does not do

          No command uplink (the sources give no high-gain receive gain or receiver noise), no low-gain antennas or attitude constraints, no DSN scheduling or arraying, no solar-cycle scaling of the Sun's noise (the expressions are the 1996 solar-minimum envelope), no Shapiro delay, no precession or polar motion in the station model (0.09 deg of elevation on average, measured against Horizons). For a spacecraft between the Earth and the Sun the scintillation model is applied at the ray's equivalent elongation, a construction of this page (module 106 is fitted to the Sun-Earth-probe angle of spacecraft beyond the Sun). The spacecraft's radio is borrowed: the chain has no spacecraft yet.
