Closing In

Idea 40 delivered the spacecraft at the asteroid with its arrival speed still on it. This page flies the last two months: the braking burns that take that speed out, a slow approach through waypoints, the asteroid's first appearance in the camera, and the spacecraft holding station near a body whose gravity is weaker than sunlight. The approach plan is OSIRIS-REx's at Bennu (AAS 19-035), and the page re-flies that approach from JPL Horizons' reconstruction to show how close it comes.

        How this works

        The forces, and the frame

        Everything is flown relative to the asteroid in non-rotating ICRF axes. The asteroid itself goes round the Sun on a conic (idea 39's Kepler propagator) from the state idea 40 handed on. On the spacecraft: the asteroid as a point mass; the Sun's tide, exactly (its pull on the spacecraft minus its pull on the asteroid); and sunlight on a flat absorber of the given area per mass, with idea 40's solar pressure (luminosity over 4π r² c), switched off in the asteroid's shadow. Integration is idea 40's Dormand-Prince 5(4), unmodified. Directions are shown in AAS 19-035's Sun Anti-Momentum frame: x toward the Sun, z against the asteroid's orbital angular momentum; solar latitude and longitude are angles in it, and the phase angle is the angle from x.

        The approach

        The arrival idea 40 hands on is aimed at the asteroid's centre at its arrival speed. Flown backwards (without the asteroid's pull, which bends a km/s path by about 1e-8 rad), it gives the incoming arc; the first braking burn is where that arc is at the chosen distance. Each burn then aims at the next waypoint, a distance, solar latitude and longitude at a time (OSIRIS-REx's, from AAS 19-035 Table 1, at the same days after its first burn; AAM-2a was waived in flight, so it is left out), solved by Newton iteration to 1 m. The last burn stops the spacecraft at the stand-off point, which is fixed in the rotating frame. Burns are impulsive for aiming; their lengths come from the rocket equation at the main engine's thrust (above 1 m/s) or the small thrusters' (below), and the distance covered while braking from the closed form for a constant-thrust burn.

        Seeing it, and knowing where it is

        The asteroid's brightness on the way in is the IAU H-G law that Horizons uses (H and G from JPL's Small-Body Database); the camera is taken to see it once it is as bright as Bennu was when OSIRIS-REx's PolyCam first imaged it (17 August 2018, V 13.08 by the same law). After that the direction to it is known to the camera's pixel (13.5 µrad) times the centroid's 0.1 pixel (AAS 19-035). Before it, the asteroid's position is as uncertain as its orbit (Horizons' 1-sigma for Apophis at the arrival) and the spacecraft's as idea 40's navigation left it. If the asteroid appears later than the time the ground needs to design a burn (5 to 7 days in AAS 19-035), the burn would be aimed blind and the page refuses it. If it is already bright where the computed arc begins, the lead shown is a lower bound (≥).

        Holding station

        A point fixed in the rotating frame needs an acceleration ω̇ × r + ω × (ω × r); the control must supply the difference between that and what gravity, the tide and sunlight give. Its size per day is the station-keeping cost. In a deadband, the spacecraft drifts across the box and is turned back at the wall it is pushed out of: a burn of 2√(2aL) every 2√(2L/a) for a box of width L. The page flies that deadband under every force and compares the cost. Near a small asteroid sunlight is stronger than gravity beyond a few kilometres, which is why the orbits that last are terminator orbits close in (OSIRIS-REx's first orbit at Bennu was 1.6 to 2.1 km, its fit below) and why holding station farther out costs thrust every day.

        The Hill sphere and the regimes

        The Hill radius d (μ/3μ☉)^(1/3) is where the Sun's tide equals the asteroid's pull on the Sun line. AAS 24-063 scales the problem by d (μ/μ☉)^(1/3) and measures sunlight by β̃; on the night side gravity balances sunlight and the tide at 1/x² = β̃ + 3x (an unstable equilibrium). That point is on the anti-Sun axis, inside the asteroid's shadow, where this page (unlike the circular ANH3BP) has no sunlight, so it is shown as AAS 24-063's number, not as a place to park. The orbit scan starts circular orbits at a range of radii in the terminator plane and in the noon-midnight plane and flies each for 30 days.

        What it does not do

        The asteroid is a point mass (its shape is module 43's). No planets: the page refuses where the Earth's tide passes 1% of the Sun's, as at Apophis in April 2029. Sunlight on a flat absorber, not OSIRIS-REx's ten-plate model with thermal re-radiation; no momentum dumps, no maneuver execution errors, no navigation filter. One specific impulse for every engine. The spacecraft is borrowed: NEAR Shoemaker's engine and tanks, OSIRIS-APEX's area per mass.
